Ancient Greece c. 150–100 BC

The Antikythera Mechanism: The Ancient Greek Computer

Divers pulled a lump of bronze from a shipwreck. Inside it were gears — and a machine a thousand years ahead of its time.

By Unseen Ages · 27 November 2026

In 1901, sponge divers working on a Roman-era shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera hauled up statues, coins and jewellery. Among them was a shapeless lump of corroded bronze and wood that nobody could identify. It turned out to be the most complex machine to survive from the ancient world: a hand-powered device of bronze gears that tracked the Sun and Moon and predicted eclipses, built roughly two thousand years ago.

What is it?

The Antikythera Mechanism is an astronomical calculator. It was housed in a wooden case about 34 by 18 by 9 centimetres — roughly the size of a shoebox — with dials on the front and back and bronze plates covered in Greek inscriptions, rather like an instruction manual.

Turning a hand crank set dozens of meshing bronze gears in motion. Pointers on the front dial showed the positions of the Sun and Moon against the zodiac, alongside a calendar. A small half-silvered ball displayed the Moon’s phases. The back dials worked through longer cycles: the 19-year Metonic calendar cycle, and the 223-month Saros cycle used to predict eclipses. One small dial even counted the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic games, including the Olympics.

How it was found

The wreck was discovered in 1900, when sponge divers from the island of Symi took shelter from a storm near Antikythera and spotted the remains of a cargo ship about 45 metres below the surface. Working with the Greek navy through 1900 and 1901, they raised its cargo of Greek statues, pottery, coins and luxury goods, probably bound for Rome when the ship sank in the first century BC.

The mechanism came up as a single encrusted lump. It was only in 1902 that the archaeologist Valerios Stais, examining the finds in Athens, noticed a gear wheel inside one of the fragments. Even then, few believed the ancient Greeks had built geared machinery. X-ray studies in the 1970s confirmed the gear trains inside, and in 2005 an international research project brought X-ray CT scanning and advanced surface imaging to Athens. The results, published in the journal Nature in 2006, doubled the amount of inscription text that could be read and revealed how much of the machine’s work the gears were doing.

What makes it special

The mechanism survives as 82 fragments holding 30 bronze gears — and the original device had more. Its largest gear is about 14 centimetres across. The teeth were cut by hand, yet the gearing is precise enough to model one of the hardest problems in ancient astronomy: the Moon does not move across the sky at a steady speed. A clever “pin-and-slot” arrangement between two gears speeds up and slows down the Moon pointer to match, mechanically reproducing a theory associated with the astronomer Hipparchus.

It is often called the world’s oldest known analogue computer. Nothing of comparable mechanical complexity is known again until the astronomical clocks built in Europe in the fourteenth century — more than a thousand years later.

What we still don’t know

We do not know who made it, or exactly when. The lettering of its inscriptions points to the second half of the second century BC, so most researchers date it to about 150–100 BC, though other dates have been proposed. The month names on its calendar belong to a Corinthian tradition, which has led some researchers to look towards Corinth’s colonies, such as Syracuse — but this is still debated.

Only about a third of the device survives, so every reconstruction involves judgement. A detailed model published by researchers at University College London in 2021 proposed how a complete display of the planets may have worked, but parts of it remain contested. Who owned the mechanism, and why it was on the ship, are also unknown.

Quick facts

  • Date: probably about 150–100 BC
  • Material: bronze gears in a wooden case, ~34 × 18 × 9 cm
  • Found: 1901, in a Roman-era shipwreck off Antikythera, Greece
  • Surviving: 82 fragments holding 30 bronze gears
  • Now kept at: National Archaeological Museum, Athens
