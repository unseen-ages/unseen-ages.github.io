The Lycurgus Cup: Rome’s Colour-Changing Glass

In ordinary light this Roman cup is jade green. Put a light behind it, and it glows blood red.

By Unseen Ages · 27 November 2026

At first glance it is simply a beautiful Roman drinking cup, carved with a scene from Greek myth. But the glass itself does something no other surviving Roman glass does. Lit from the front, the cup looks opaque jade green. Lit from behind, the same glass turns a deep, glowing red. For decades after it reached the British Museum, nobody could explain why.

What is it?

The Lycurgus Cup was made in the fourth century AD, probably between about 290 and 325, possibly in Rome or Alexandria. It is a “cage cup”: Roman craftsmen started with a thick blank of glass and painstakingly cut and ground it back until the decoration stood out like a cage, joined to the cup’s body only by tiny hidden bridges of glass.

The scene shows King Lycurgus of Thrace, who attacked the wine god Dionysus and was punished by being strangled in a tangle of grapevines. Most cage cups carry simple geometric patterns. This one carries figures, and it is the only well-preserved figural cage cup known. It stands about 15.9 centimetres tall and 13.2 centimetres across.

How it reached the museum

The cup’s early history is unknown — nobody recorded where it spent its first fifteen centuries. It appears in records in the nineteenth century and later belonged to the Rothschild family. The British Museum acquired it in 1958, and its strange behaviour in different light soon became famous among glass specialists.

The colour puzzle lasted for decades. It was solved in 1990, when researchers D. J. Barber and Ian Freestone examined tiny fragments of the glass under a transmission electron microscope — and found the answer hiding at a scale far too small for any ordinary microscope.

What makes it special

The glass is full of minute particles of silver and gold, about 50 to 100 nanometres across — less than one-thousandth the size of a grain of table salt, as Smithsonian Magazine puts it. There is very little metal in total, only a few hundred parts per million, but the particles change how the glass handles light. When light hits them, the electrons in the metal vibrate in ways that filter the colours: green dominates when light reflects off the surface, while reds and oranges pass through when light shines from behind. The effect is called dichroism.

In other words, Roman craftsmen were working with what we now call nanoparticles, some 1,600 years before the word existed. Freestone has described the precision of the metal mixture as “an amazing feat”.

The cup still inspires new work. In 2013, engineers at the University of Illinois built an experimental sensor on the same principle — a plastic plate covered with billions of tiny nanoparticle-coated wells — and reported it was far more sensitive to changes in salt solutions than commercial sensors. And in October 2026, a team at the University of Sheffield announced that it had finally recreated the cup’s colour-changing glass, after long trial and error in the furnace.

What we still don’t know

The biggest question is whether the effect was deliberate. The metal mixture is suspiciously precise, which suggests skilled control. But no Roman text describes the recipe, and no second cup does the same trick — this is the only complete surviving example of dichroic glass from the ancient world. It is possible that the effect began as a lucky accident in one workshop, understood just well enough to be repeated once.

We also do not know who owned the cup, where exactly it was made, or how it survived intact while almost every other piece of Roman luxury glass broke. The cup keeps its colour secret in plain sight: it only performs when the light is right.

Quick facts

  • Date: 4th century AD, probably about 290–325
  • Material: dichroic glass — a silver-gold alloy in particles of ~50–100 nanometres
  • Type: cage cup (diatretum), carved from a single thick glass blank
  • Size: 15.9 cm tall, 13.2 cm across
  • Now kept at: British Museum, London (acquired 1958)
