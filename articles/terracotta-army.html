The Terracotta Army: 8,000 Soldiers of Clay

They were digging a well during a drought. Instead of water, the farmers found a soldier — the first of thousands.

By Unseen Ages · 27 November 2026

In March 1974, in Lintong County outside Xi’an, a group of farmers — Yang Zhifa, his five brothers and their neighbour Wang Puzhi — were digging a well about a kilometre and a half from a great burial mound. Their shovels struck fired clay: fragments of a life-size figure, and bronze arrowheads. They had no idea they had just made one of the greatest archaeological discoveries of the twentieth century.

What is it?

The Terracotta Army is a vast collection of life-size clay figures buried in pits near the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, the man who unified China and proclaimed himself its first emperor in 221 BC. The figures were made to guard and serve him in the afterlife.

Archaeologists estimate that the three main pits hold more than 8,000 soldiers, along with 130 chariots with 520 horses and 150 cavalry horses — and most of them are still underground. The soldiers stand in battle formation, and they are not copies of one model: their heights vary with rank, from about 184 to 197 centimetres, the generals tallest of all, and their faces, hairstyles and uniforms were individually finished. They once carried real bronze weapons, and they were originally painted in bright colours.

How it was found

The farmers reported their strange find, and archaeologists soon began to uncover the pits. What emerged was far bigger than an army. The pits are only one part of a huge necropolis built like a mirror of the emperor’s capital, covering more than 56 square kilometres around his tomb mound, with nearly 200 accompanying pits and over 600 sites in the protected area.

Work on the mausoleum had begun in 246 BC, soon after the future emperor came to the throne of Qin as a boy of thirteen. The historian Sima Qian, writing about a century later, described a vast underground palace — and claimed that models of rivers were made with flowing mercury. Remarkably, modern soil tests around the mound have found unusually high levels of mercury, which may support his account.

The discovery site is now protected by museum halls built over the pits, and in 1987 UNESCO added the Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor to the World Heritage List.

What makes it special

The scale is only half the story. The warriors were produced in workshops that combined something like mass production — bodies built from standard parts — with individual hand-finishing, so that no two faces are exactly alike. Their uniforms, armour and hairstyles record the organisation of the Qin army in extraordinary detail, down to the horses’ halters, as UNESCO’s listing notes.

The paint tells a sadder story. The figures were once vividly coloured, but the ancient paint begins to flake and fade within minutes of exposure to air once a figure is excavated. That is one reason archaeologists now dig slowly and cautiously, and why so much of the army remains deliberately buried.

What we still don’t know

The emperor’s own tomb chamber, beneath the great mound, has never been opened. Archaeologists have chosen not to excavate it, partly to protect whatever is inside — and Sima Qian’s description of mercury rivers, backed by those soil readings, is another reason for care. What the chamber holds remains one of archaeology’s great unopened doors.

There are open questions above ground, too: how the pits were damaged and burned in the chaos after the Qin dynasty fell, how many figures the unexcavated areas contain, and how to preserve the paint on figures yet to be uncovered. Two thousand years after they were buried, the clay soldiers are still standing guard — and still keeping secrets.

Quick facts

  • Buried: about 210–209 BC, with Qin Shi Huang, China’s first emperor
  • Estimated figures: more than 8,000 soldiers, 130 chariots, 670 horses across three pits
  • Found: 29 March 1974, by farmers digging a well at Lintong, near Xi’an
  • Heights: about 184–197 cm, varying by rank
  • Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987; the emperor’s tomb chamber remains unopened
