The Tarkhan Dress: The World’s Oldest Woven Garment

A 5,000-year-old linen shirt sat unnoticed in a pile of rags for 64 years.

By Unseen Ages · 27 November 2026

It is a small, pale linen shirt with a V-neck and finely pleated sleeves. It looks simple, almost modern. Yet scientists have dated it to more than five thousand years ago, which makes the Tarkhan Dress the oldest woven garment known anywhere in the world. Even more surprising: for decades, nobody knew it existed, even though it had already been dug up.

What is the Tarkhan Dress?

The Tarkhan Dress is a linen top made from three pieces of hand-woven cloth with a faint grey stripe. The bodice and sleeves are knife-pleated, and the neckline is cut in a V shape. The bottom edge, or hem, is missing, so we do not know how long it once was.

Today it belongs to the Petrie Museum of Egyptian Archaeology at University College London (UCL). It is not just old cloth wrapped around a body. It is a cut, fitted and sewn piece of clothing, which is why experts call it the earliest surviving example of tailored woven clothing.

How it was found

In 1912 and 1913, the British archaeologist Flinders Petrie excavated a large ancient cemetery at Tarkhan, about 50 kilometres south of Cairo. One of the tombs, a mud-brick structure known as mastaba 2050, had been robbed long before. Among what remained was a big pile of old linen cloth.

The linen was sent to London and stored away. For more than sixty years, no one looked closely at it. Then, in 1977, the bundle went to the Victoria and Albert Museum for cleaning and conservation. While sorting through many pieces of different textiles, the conservators realised that one of them was not a rag at all. It was a complete upper garment, with its pleats still visible.

The team carefully cleaned it and stitched it onto a fine, transparent silk support so it could be displayed safely. From a forgotten bundle of “funerary rags”, the shirt suddenly became one of the most important textiles in the world.

How old is it really?

Because the tomb had been looted in ancient times, the exact age of the dress was debated for years. Linen found with it was tested in the 1980s, but the dress itself was too precious to cut a large sample from.

In 2015, as part of the Petrie Museum’s centenary, the museum’s curator worked with the University of Oxford’s radiocarbon unit. They removed a tiny thread weighing just 2.24 milligrams. Linen is good for radiocarbon dating because flax plants grow and die in a short time. The result placed the dress between 3482 and 3102 BC, with 95% probability.

That means the shirt may be older than Egypt’s First Dynasty, and it is centuries older than the Great Pyramid of Giza, which was built around 2560 BC.

Was it actually worn?

Experts who studied the dress noted creases around the elbows and armpits, the kind of folds that form when clothing is worn. That suggests it was used in life and not made only for the tomb. Its small size suggests it may have belonged to a slim young woman or a teenager, though we cannot know exactly who wore it.

What we still don’t know

We do not know the owner’s name, how long the garment originally was, or whether it was everyday clothing or something special. Because the tomb was disturbed, the dress was found outside its original burial. Each answer we have comes from the fabric itself.

What we do know is remarkable enough: more than five thousand years ago, someone in Egypt wove linen, cut it to shape, pleated it by hand, and wore it. Their shirt survived when almost everything else from their world did not.

Quick facts

  • Date: 3482–3102 BC (radiocarbon, 95% probability)
  • Material: hand-woven linen, three pieces, knife-pleated
  • Found: 1913 by Flinders Petrie at Tarkhan, ~50 km south of Cairo
  • Recognised as a garment: 1977, during conservation at the V&A
  • Now kept at: Petrie Museum of Egyptian Archaeology, UCL, London
