Tutankhamun’s Meteorite Dagger: A Blade from Space

The boy king was buried with an iron dagger — in an age before Egypt could make iron. Its metal fell from the sky.

By Unseen Ages · 27 November 2026

When Howard Carter unwrapped the mummy of Tutankhamun in 1925, he found two daggers folded into the linen. One had a blade of gold. The other — laid against the king’s right thigh — had a blade of iron, a metal rarer than gold in Bronze Age Egypt, and it had barely rusted in more than three thousand years. Nearly a century later, scientists proved where that iron came from: a meteorite.

What is it?

The dagger is about 35 centimetres long in total, with a finely made iron blade of roughly 22 centimetres, a gold hilt decorated with granulation and coloured inlay, and a pommel of rock crystal. Its gold sheath is decorated on one side with a lily motif and on the other with a pattern of feathers ending in a jackal’s head.

Tutankhamun reigned in the fourteenth century BC, long before iron smelting was established in Egypt. Iron objects were rare and precious — which is why a king was buried with one at his thigh. Egyptians had, however, worked iron that fell from the sky much earlier: iron beads made from meteorite metal, found at Gerzeh, date to well over a thousand years before Tutankhamun’s time.

How it was found

Carter discovered Tutankhamun’s tomb in the Valley of the Kings in 1922 — the most famous archaeological find of the century. The daggers came to light three years later, during the careful unwrapping of the mummy in 1925.

From the 1970s, some researchers suspected the blade was meteoritic, because early tests found a high nickel content — a classic fingerprint of iron meteorites. But a 1994 analysis measured only about 2.8% nickel and concluded the opposite, and the question stayed open for another twenty years.

What the tests showed

In 2016, an Italian-Egyptian team led by Daniela Comelli examined the blade at the Egyptian Museum in Cairo with a portable X-ray fluorescence spectrometer — a method that reads the metal’s chemistry without taking any sample or causing damage. The result: the blade is iron with 10.8% nickel and 0.58% cobalt, and the ratio between the two matches iron meteorites closely. The team concluded that the composition “strongly supports its meteoritic origin”.

A second study in 2022, by Takafumi Matsui and colleagues, mapped the blade’s surface chemistry in detail. They found a faint cross-hatched pattern in the metal — a Widmanstätten pattern, which forms inside iron meteorites as they cool in space over millions of years. Because that pattern is destroyed by high heat, its survival shows the blade was shaped by careful low-temperature forging, below about 950 °C.

What we still don’t know

Which meteorite supplied the metal is unknown. The 2016 team compared the blade with meteorites found within 2,000 kilometres of Egypt’s Red Sea coast and found one, named Kharga, with a similar composition — but “similar” is not proof, and the 2022 pattern evidence may point to a different kind of meteorite.

Even stranger: the 2022 team found that the glue under the hilt’s decorations appears to be lime plaster, a material not used in Egypt until many centuries later. They suggest the dagger may have been made abroad and given as a royal gift — possibly from the kingdom of Mitanni. Remarkably, one of the Amarna letters, the diplomatic correspondence of the time, records that the king of Mitanni sent an iron dagger with a gold hilt to Amenhotep III, Tutankhamun’s grandfather. Whether that gift is this dagger, nobody can yet say.

Quick facts

  • Buried: about 1323 BC, in Tutankhamun’s tomb, Valley of the Kings
  • Blade: meteoritic iron — 10.8% nickel, 0.58% cobalt (2016 X-ray study)
  • Length: about 35 cm in total; blade about 22 cm
  • Found: 1925 by Howard Carter, in the wrappings of the mummy
  • Now kept at: Grand Egyptian Museum, Giza
