Göbekli Tepe: A Temple Older Than Farming

Stone circles raised about 11,500 years ago — some 6,000 years before Stonehenge — by people who did not yet farm.

By Unseen Ages · 27 November 2026

On a limestone hill in south-eastern Türkiye, near the city of Şanlıurfa, stand rings of enormous carved stone pillars. They were raised around 9600 BC — roughly 11,500 years ago. That is about 6,000 years before Stonehenge and 7,000 years before the Great Pyramid of Egypt. And the people who built them were hunter-gatherers, living before farming, before pottery, before metal tools, before writing.

What is it?

Göbekli Tepe is a complex of monumental enclosures built during the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, between about 9600 and 8200 BC. The oldest structures are great circular enclosures of stone walls, into which builders set huge T-shaped limestone pillars, some up to 5.5 metres tall and weighing several tonnes, all quarried and carved with stone tools.

The T-shape is not abstract. The pillars are stylised human figures: some carry carved arms, hands, belts and loincloths, and many are decorated with reliefs of wild animals — foxes, boars, snakes, cranes and scorpions among them. Two taller pillars usually stand at the centre of each ring. UNESCO, which added the site to the World Heritage List in 2018, describes the enclosures as monumental communal buildings, probably used in connection with social events and rituals.

How it was found

The hill was not exactly lost. A joint survey by Istanbul University and the University of Chicago noted the site in 1963, but its carved stones were not recognised for what they were, and the hill was largely dismissed as a medieval burial ground.

The turning point came in 1994, when the German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt visited the site and realised that the stone fragments scattered on the surface belonged to something far older and far bigger. Excavations began in 1995 under Schmidt and the German Archaeological Institute, together with the Şanlıurfa Museum. Schmidt directed the dig until his death in 2014; the work continues today as a Turkish-German collaboration. It was Schmidt who argued that the enclosures were among the world’s first temples — a sanctuary built by hunter-gatherers who gathered there from across the region.

What makes it special

Göbekli Tepe reversed a famous story. The old assumption was that people first settled down, farmed, and grew surpluses — and only then had the organisation and spare time to build monuments and temples. Here, monument-building appears to come first, carried out by foragers who hunted gazelle and gathered wild plants. Some researchers think the work of feeding and organising large building crews may itself have encouraged the first steps towards farming in this region — the area where some of the world’s earliest farming communities emerged.

The scale of cooperation is the marvel: quarrying, carving, transporting and raising multi-tonne pillars, and feeding the workforce, all without wheels, metal or domesticated animals. UNESCO calls the site one of the first manifestations of human-made monumental architecture.

What we still don’t know

A great deal. Only about a tenth of the site has been excavated, so most of the hill is still unread. The enclosures were eventually filled in with rubble; whether that was a deliberate burial or a slower process is debated. Recent excavations have also found remains of what appear to be domestic buildings, so the picture of a pure, empty sanctuary is evolving — people may have lived at Göbekli Tepe as well as gathered there.

Above all, we do not know what the builders believed. The animal carvings, the human-like pillars and the rituals hinted at by the enclosures are a language we can only partly read. What is certain is that 11,500 years ago, on a bare hill in Anatolia, Stone Age hunters did something nobody thought they could do — and we are still catching up with them.

Quick facts

  • Date: about 9600–8200 BC (Pre-Pottery Neolithic)
  • Material: T-shaped limestone pillars, up to 5.5 m tall, carved with stone tools
  • Builders: hunter-gatherer communities of Upper Mesopotamia
  • Recognised: 1994 by Klaus Schmidt; excavations since 1995 (Şanlıurfa Museum + German Archaeological Institute)
  • Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2018; only about a tenth excavated
